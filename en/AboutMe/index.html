Work Experience

  1. 2020.06 - 2021.04
  • Beijing Xinglan Technology Co., Ltd.
    This was a really impressive Tsinghua startup, with a stellar founding team (Blue-lotus + r3kapig + aliyun).
    In 2025, while in the hospital, I heard the company was gone. Such a shame — when I can return to Beijing, I won’t even have a place to clock in.
    Summary:
    I don’t have a particularly deep impression of this period. In 2020, I joined this company I didn’t know much about. After a few days, all I remember was realizing I was a nobody with zero technical background, surrounded by brothers who were each more outstanding than the last.
    Back then everyone was on the same Wi-Fi, and all the servers were on the same subnet. As an ops engineer — a slightly obsessive-compulsive one — I dug an ordinary home router out of the warehouse, bought some network cables and RJ45 connectors, made some patch cables, and wired up the servers. In the end I successfully isolated the subnet of regular colleagues from that of the offensive/defensive security colleagues.
    Day by day, in the R&D department, I worked on that honeypot with the only remaining brother, Lao Liu — I did the frontend, he did the backend. We worked together, went down for lunch together, and gradually became good buddies.
    I forget which month it was, probably November, when the company moved from Yingu Tower to Suzhou Street’s Yinke Tower. I actually liked Yingu a lot (I lived in Qingta Xili then, and bus 611 took me straight there in the morning). After moving to Yinke, I had to move to Yongle West in Shijingshan — which just happened to land me in the soon-to-collapse Danke apartment. hahahaha. From then on, my daily commute was walking a bit, then bus 76, transfer to Line 1, then Line 10 to Suzhou Street, then walk.

The office network at Yinke Tower was set up by me. As a non-professional network engineer, can you imagine an R&D guy whose daily work was building an office network with eNSP, two switches, and an AC? After testing it many times in the simulator, I finally configured the office network successfully on moving day. I remember before I could even test it, the power went out — gg. My professional integrity as an ops engineer drove me to stay in the server room overnight alone and test the network and ACL until everything was OK before leaving. All I remember is that I was so dizzy from staring at the screen that when I got out of the building I couldn’t tell east from west and couldn’t even find a ride. I wobbled home and slept until the afternoon.

The company made a few strange moves in 2021.
The product I was working on: on one hand, they sent me to customer sites for implementation (it was hilarious — I pretended to implement at the customer’s site, and when I hit a problem I’d say “let me note it down and report back to R&D”, then when I got back I’d fix my own part myself). On the other hand, they wouldn’t let me go, telling me these trial customers were arranged by sales as favors without company approval. The result was that I often agreed on a time with someone and then was forced to stand them up. The thing I hate most is deliberately standing people up.
After a few rounds of this, I couldn’t take it anymore and resigned. I cleaned up my stuff at the company, deleted my account, closed permissions — I killed myself, which was kind of fun. Once the approval went through, I grabbed my things and left, though I didn’t have much.

  1. 2021.07.01 - 2024.04.19
  • NSFOCUS
    Department: Threat Intelligence R&D
    This is where I stayed the longest, and where I found the work style most comfortable. When I first arrived, facing a server room full of machines, I was a bit overwhelmed, but after an afternoon of getting familiar, I could get my hands on it.
    In the early days, it was mainly initializing a batch of new machines. The daily work of taking apart and installing hard drives at my desk wasn’t bad. The 740xd isn’t as easy to install drives into as the earlier 730/730xd. Installing the OS was more troublesome, so I set up a PXE server in the server room — there was actually a Cobbler already, but I built my own mainly because I wasn’t familiar with it. I’d move machines with the same role (like all workers and masters) together, plug them in, power on, switch in KVM, select the boot item, and then leave. When I came back after lunch, the system was installed. Before leaving work in the evening, I’d finish configuring the machines. I forget which day they were shipped out to be racked at a remote site.
    Back then, both the R&D server room and the production server room had frequent disk failures — RAID showing failure directly, or SMART error counts that were horrifying. I was lucky enough to witness a RAID5 with two failed disks. We piled the removed bad disks on the balcony next to the big boss’s office. Later when we moved desks, the bad hard drives filled two whole boxes (the kind used for office desktop computers).
    Since it’s a security company, to better fit into the corporate culture, I started participating in some CTFs. With no real skills, I could only do traffic analysis, social engineering, and programming challenges. For reversing and pwn, I occasionally managed to solve one or two simple ones.

In 2022, our department went through a personnel adjustment. At the time, I had an abscess on my sacrum and was lying in the hospital for a week after surgery. I remember lying there receiving a bunch of machines that had been transferred to me from my leader. After I was discharged, I had to go to the hospital every day to change dressings. A few days later, my roommate got a pop-up saying to quarantine at home, so I was forced to stay home with him for two weeks.

Then our team basically stabilized. That team of just over ten people supported NTI and NTIP, steady and stable. I was responsible for infrastructure, occasionally helping with threat intelligence analysis. The brothers handled the business. Everything looked promising.

On May 17, 2023, while riding my bike to work, I crashed into a shared bike at Huayuan Bridge, then got rear-ended and my foot slipped when I landed, twisting it. I thought it was nothing and let the guy go. I sat by the roadside for half a day and still couldn’t stand up; my foot hurt badly.
A buddy took a taxi to get me to the hospital. A CT scan showed a talus fracture. I waited outside the ER for my roommate to come and help with the admission procedures. I stayed in the hospital for five or six days. After the swelling went down, the doctor said surgery wasn’t needed, so I got a cast and went home. The first time climbing the stairs at home was exhausting; after that it was okay.

I worked from home for a month, remotely accessing the company computer every day to handle issues. When I wanted to slack off, I’d bring my laptop to the desktop and fire up a game — work when there’s work, have fun when there’s none.

Afterward: the brothers I worked with were gradually laid off. I took over an operations system that was still under development. I can only say that it’s fortunate I majored in C++ back in school, so picking up my brothers’ Java code wasn’t too hard. I could sort of fix the early bugs.

Later we took on a project with a demanding environment. Fortunately I could still handle it: Hadoop, ARM, KVM, S3 storage.

Next, I held on until April 19th, when I got my severance package and was kicked out. That ended my time at NSFOCUS.
I wonder if the Zaoyang baozi place at Yitai’s entrance and the Qianhou Shuanrou restaurant downstairs from Qingdong are still open.
Speaking of Qianhou Shuanrou, it’s our department’s tradition — departing colleagues would take everyone out for a meal. There were exceptions. I remember before Brother Kuang left, the department had a meal at Century Golden Resources. I don’t know how many bottles we went through, but we drank happily.
My buddy and I rode our bikes there and pushed them back to the company.

  1. 2024.11.04 - 2025.01.29
  • Adecco - Alimama
    Before receiving this offer, I’d interviewed a lot. Either I wasn’t prepared enough and got rejected in the first or second round, or the location was too far.
    Actually, my principle for job hunting is simple: whoever sends the offer first wins. Job hunting is a two-way choice — if you approve of me, I’ll choose you.
    There were also cases where the final approval didn’t pass. The one I wanted most among all the companies I interviewed with was Sina. I did an on-site interview, took a taxi from Shijingshan, and didn’t even mention reimbursing the fare. After the interview, I felt the work content matched my interests and development plans well. But the approval didn’t pass.

Alibaba was too far from where I lived — 56 subway stops round trip every day. It was tiring at first, but once I got into the work rhythm, I got used to it.
The work at Alibaba was model-related.
Mainly producing performance stress-testing metrics for models.
I cobbled together a web service (nothing technical, just pure HTML+JS, and the UI wasn’t great) to run that stress-testing process, with the help of GPT and Qianwen.
To be honest, I didn’t do a great job — I took shortcuts with tools and AI-generated APIs that had a bunch of default-value bugs.
And that’s how I worked until 2025.

On the morning of the first day of Chinese New Year, I had a cerebral hemorrhage of 53ML. I got on the ambulance, closed my eyes, and knew nothing. When I woke up, I was on my way to another hospital. By the time I was clear-headed and knew who I was and where I was, Lantern Festival had just passed.

Damn, I’d bought a ticket back to Beijing for the fourth day of the New Year.
Luckily I wasn’t brain-dead and still remembered my phone password — unlocked it and requested leave.
The rest you can guess: after my medical leave ran out and I couldn’t work, I left automatically.
Speaking of which, Adecco was actually pretty decent — they were willing to keep paying my health insurance until I could barely walk on my own.
Arigato.
