An Incident Recall

While browsing my phone’s photo album in the afternoon, I found a picture:

I couldn’t recall what happened that day. I forgot what I was doing at the network center all night — maybe a course project, maybe a minor upgrade of the IDP server deployed at school. I can’t remember how I found out I had been blacklisted. At first I thought my IDP server had been hacked and had a webshell planted on it, so I checked everything and dug through the whole server without finding anything suspicious, then filed an appeal. The response said a website under another domain of the school had malicious content — an administrative business website. I opened it and pressed F12: there was a long chunk of VBScript that wrote a piece of data in binary.

So I grabbed it. Why wasn’t I afraid of getting infected? Because in my junior and senior years my computer ran Linux. Why Linux? I was learning Linux programming and CUDA at the time. Under Linux, NVIDIA provided nsight, plus the CUDA C IDE: Nsight Eclipse Edition, for developing and debugging programs on the GPU. My poor 860M only had 2GB of VRAM, and it was even used to train models for my graduation project…

MaxStack Verification in NTIP Customer Environment

Problem Statement

The upgrade failed at the customer site. The failing step was calling decrypt to unpack the upgrade package.
It runs fine in the company environment, but segfaults in the customer environment.

1 How It Was Found

Since there was no source code for decrypt, I checked the binary — the symbol table was not stripped, so I loaded it into IDA. The original plan was just to set breakpoints based on the call stack.

IDA reported an error in the verify_encrypted_packet_sha256 function:
stack frame too big

I immediately realized it was a stack limit issue — intuition, I guess.
